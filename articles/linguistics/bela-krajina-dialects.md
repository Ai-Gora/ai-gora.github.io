Bela Krajina has two Slovene dialects, North Bela Krajina and South Bela Krajina, and both belong to the Lower Carniolan dialect group.[^1][^2]

## Overview {#overview}

Bela Krajina (White Carniola) lies between the Gorjanci hills to the north, the Kočevje forests to the west and the Kolpa river, which forms the border with Croatia.[^2]
Its local speech is divided into two dialects: the North Bela Krajina dialect (*severnobelokranjsko narečje*) and the South Bela Krajina dialect (*južnobelokranjsko narečje*).[^2]
Speech in the region mixes Slovene, Croatian and Serbian elements in different amounts.[^2]
For Slovenia's official administrative and statistical units, see [[eu-administrative-divisions#slovenia|administrative divisions in the EU]].

## Classification {#classification}

The official Map of Slovene dialects is published by the Fran Ramovš Institute of the Slovenian Language at ZRC SAZU.[^1]
Its 2023 version builds on the 1983 map by Tine Logar and Jakob Rigler.[^1]
The map puts both Bela Krajina dialects in the Lower Carniolan dialect group (*dolenjska narečna skupina*).[^1]
The same group also includes the Lower Carniolan dialect, the Kostel dialect and the mixed Kočevje speech.[^1]
The institute's chart is usually summarised as 48 dialects and 13 subdialects of Slovene.[^3]

## North Bela Krajina dialect {#north}

The northern dialect is spoken around Semič, Črnomelj and Metlika.[^2]
Its southern border runs roughly along a line from Jelševnik (near Dobliče) to Krasinec (near Podzemelj), as drawn by the dialectologist Tine Logar.[^2]
North of this line, the old long and old acute vowels developed in the same way as in Lower Carniola and other central dialects.[^2]
Its short vowels were also reduced, as in most Slovene dialects.[^2]
Shtokavian (Serbo-Croatian) influence in this dialect is smaller than in the south.[^2]

## South Bela Krajina dialect {#south}

The southern dialect is spoken south of the Jelševnik–Krasinec line.[^2]
There, the old acute vowels stayed short, as in Croatian and Serbian.[^2]
Most southern local dialects did not go through the modern Slovene vowel reduction.[^2]
Some of them, for example in Dobliče, Dragatuš, Vinica and Stari trg ob Kolpi, keep a non-Slovene type of pitch accent.[^2]
Many southern local dialects are mixed or have a non-Slovene base.[^2]

## Shtokavian villages {#shtokavian}

After Ottoman raids in the 15th and 16th centuries, many local people moved north, and Uskoks and other settlers from Lika, Bosnia and Dalmatia arrived.[^2]
Logar found three kinds of local speech in the region: purely or mostly Slovene, mixed Slovene–Croatian, and purely Croatian or Serbian.[^2]
Shtokavian speech islands are clearest along the Kolpa, in Bojanci, Marindol, Miliči and Paunoviči.[^2]
In these villages Serbo-Croatian was used as a school language until the 1960s.[^2]

## History of classification {#history}

In 1931 the linguist Fran Ramovš divided Bela Krajina speech into four dialects on a Lower Carniolan base.[^2]
In 1935 he described the same varieties as local dialects (*govori*) rather than full dialects.[^2]
He called them mixed Slovene–Serbo-Croatian dialects, with more Slovene in the north and more Serbo-Croatian in the south.[^2]
Logar later drew the north–south border that is still used today.[^2]

## Standard language {#standard}

The first two printed books in Slovene, Primož Trubar's *Catechismus* and *Abecedarium*, appeared in 1550.[^4]
On how print shaped written languages, see [[printing-press#effects|effects of printing]].

## Open questions {#open-questions}

The border between the Bela Krajina dialects is described as not clearly defined.[^2]
How the local dialects right on the Kolpa relate to the neighbouring Croatian Kajkavian dialects has not yet been researched.[^2]

## Key claims {#key-claims}

- Bela Krajina has two Slovene dialects: North and South Bela Krajina.[^1][^2]
- Both are in the Lower Carniolan dialect group on the official ZRC SAZU map.[^1]
- The border between them runs roughly from Jelševnik to Krasinec.[^2]
- Serbo-Croatian influence is much stronger in the southern dialect.[^2]
- Bojanci, Marindol, Miliči and Paunoviči are Shtokavian-speaking villages.[^2]
- The first printed Slovene books appeared in 1550.[^4]
